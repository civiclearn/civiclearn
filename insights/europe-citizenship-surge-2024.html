1.2 Million New Europeans: Inside the 2024 Citizenship Surge

Eurostat’s freshest numbers show that EU countries granted citizenship to more people in 2024 than in any year on record. Germany’s law reform lit the fuse, Sweden led the naturalisation rate, Denmark’s numbers nearly doubled — and behind every data point is a test that somebody had to pass.

Three countries — Germany, Spain, and Italy — accounted for nearly two-thirds of all EU citizenships granted in 2024. Data: Eurostat migr_acq, March 2026.

On 27 March 2026, Eurostat published its annual citizenship acquisition data, and the headline number deserves a moment of stillness before the analysis begins: in 2024, EU member states granted citizenship to 1,177,232 people. That is nearly 1.2 million new Europeans — roughly the population of Prague, or twice the population of Luxembourg — minted in a single calendar year.

This was not a one-year spike. The number has been climbing for a decade. In 2014, when Eurostat began this data series in its current form, EU countries naturalised 762,100 people. The 2024 figure represents a 54.5 percent increase over that baseline. But the year-on-year acceleration was also remarkable: 122,700 more people acquired citizenship in 2024 than in 2023, an 11.6 percent jump driven largely by one country and one law.

•   •   •

I. The German Effect

Germany alone accounted for 288,700 new citizenships in 2024 — nearly a quarter of the EU total. That figure was 88,900 higher than the previous year, a 44.5 percent increase that dwarfed every other member state’s absolute change. The reason has a specific date: 27 June 2024.

On that day, Germany’s modernised nationality law took effect, cutting the minimum residency requirement for naturalisation from eight years to five and — critically — permitting dual citizenship as a matter of course. Previously, most applicants had been forced to renounce their existing nationality to become German. The new law removed that barrier, opening the door for hundreds of thousands of long-term residents, particularly from Turkey, Syria, and the Western Balkans, who had been eligible for years but unwilling to surrender their birth citizenship.

Germany’s 2024 Law Reform

Residency: Reduced from 8 years to 5 (3 in exceptional cases, since rescinded in October 2025)

Dual citizenship: Now permitted unconditionally — no more renunciation requirement

Guest worker generation: Simplified language requirements; no citizenship test needed

Impact: +88,900 citizenships year-on-year (+44.5%)

The Syrians were the largest single beneficiary group across the EU as a whole: 110,100 new citizenships, of which 75.6 percent were granted by Germany. Many of these individuals arrived during the 2015–2016 migration surge and had, by 2024, met the residency threshold. With dual citizenship now permitted, the last major obstacle had fallen.

For those naturalising in Germany, the citizenship test — Einbürgerungstest — remains a requirement for most applicants. It consists of 33 questions drawn from a publicly available pool of 310, covering German history, the constitution, democratic values, and the specific federal state of residence. A pass requires 17 correct answers. With the reformed law came a flood of applications and, as immigration lawyers across Berlin and Munich have reported, months-long waits simply to book a test appointment.

II. The Nordic Divergence

The data reveals a striking split in Europe’s north.

Sweden recorded the highest naturalisation rate in the entire EU: 7.5 citizenships granted per 100 non-national residents. That rate is nearly double the EU average of 2.7 and more than triple Germany’s. Sweden granted 63,000 new citizenships in total, placing it fifth in the EU despite having a population of just 10.5 million.

This result reflects Sweden’s historically accessible path to citizenship. There is no formal knowledge test. No civics exam. No language exam as a legal precondition for naturalisation (though language proficiency is typically assessed informally). The contrast with neighbouring Denmark is difficult to overstate, as we have documented in our comparative analysis of Nordic testing philosophies.

But here is the twist: Denmark recorded the largest relative increase of any EU country in 2024 — a staggering 86.4 percent jump. This was not because Denmark had relaxed its famously stringent requirements. The indfødsretsprøve remains a 45-question, 45-minute ordeal with an 80 percent pass threshold and a separate values gate, and as we have explored in depth, it still defeats more than half its candidates. The increase appears to reflect Denmark’s parliamentary naturalisation process: citizenship is granted by statute, bundled into naturalisation bills that pass through the Folketing in batches. A larger-than-usual bill, combined with a growing pool of eligible residents who had cleared the test in previous sittings, pushed the 2024 number sharply upward.

Sweden grants 7.5 citizenships per 100 non-nationals. Lithuania grants 0.1. The gap between the EU’s most open door and its most closed is not a spectrum — it is a chasm.

Finland, where CivicLearn has recently launched its trilingual citizenship test preparation, does not feature prominently in the absolute numbers. But Finland’s own testing architecture — the YKI language exam, with its B1 threshold — is part of a tightening trend across the Nordics. The days of test-free naturalisation may be numbered even in Stockholm. The Swedish government has signalled repeatedly that a language requirement is coming; it is a question of when, not if.

III. The Mediterranean Pattern

Spain and Italy together granted 469,900 citizenships — nearly 40 percent of the EU total. Their paths to citizenship are fundamentally different from the test-heavy Nordic and Germanic models.

Spain’s route is residency-based: ten years of legal residence for most applicants, reduced to two years for nationals of Latin American countries, Equatorial Guinea, the Philippines, Portugal, and Andorra — a legacy of colonial ties. The Spanish test, the CCSE (Conocimientos Constitucionales y Socioculturales de España), was introduced only in 2015 and is administered by the Instituto Cervantes. It is, by European standards, moderate in difficulty: 25 questions, 60 percent pass mark, broadly focused on the Spanish constitution and everyday cultural knowledge.

Italy’s approach has historically been more administrative than examination-based, though a B1-level Italian language certificate has been required since 2018. The 217,400 citizenships granted in 2024 placed Italy third in the EU, with Albanians (65.9 percent of Albanian naturalisations EU-wide) and Moroccans (28.5 percent) as the dominant recipient groups.

France, at 103,700 citizenships (8.8 percent of the EU total), represents a different tradition entirely. As we reported in our analysis of the 2026 examen civique, France has been transitioning from an interview-based assessment to a formal written examination — one of the most philosophically ambitious in Europe, blending constitutional knowledge with scenario-based situational questions. The 2024 numbers predate the new exam format, but France’s trajectory is clear: more structure, more standardisation, and higher expectations.

The Syrian dominance of the list is, at this point, a structural feature rather than a headline: these are the 2015 arrivals reaching the end of their residency timers. The Moroccan presence reflects deep historical ties with Spain, France, and Italy. The Romanian figure is notable because these are EU citizens acquiring citizenship of a different EU country — a reminder that intra-EU mobility is itself a pipeline to naturalisation.

Across the EU, 88 percent of new citizens came from outside the bloc. Citizens of other EU countries accounted for 10.6 percent. The median age was 31.7 years. Women slightly outnumbered men (50.9 to 49.1 percent), and nearly a quarter of all new citizens were children under 15 — a reminder that citizenship, in many cases, is a family event.

V. The Naturalisation Rate: A Fairer Lens

Raw numbers can mislead. Germany granted the most citizenships, but its naturalisation rate — the number of citizenships per 100 non-national residents — was not even in the top five. This is because Germany is home to nearly 13 million non-nationals, by far the largest foreign-resident population in the EU.

The naturalisation rate reshuffles the ranking entirely:

The floor of the table is as instructive as the ceiling. Lithuania’s 0.1 rate means that for every 1,000 non-nationals living in the country, only one acquired citizenship in 2024. Estonia’s 0.3 rate reflects a legacy issue unique to the Baltics: a large Russian-speaking population with “undetermined citizenship” status — not quite stateless, not quite nationals — for whom the path to Estonian citizenship involves both a language exam and a constitutional knowledge test, conducted in Estonian.

Luxembourg, though small in absolute terms, had the highest citizenship-to-population ratio at 8.8 per 1,000 total residents (a different metric from the naturalisation rate). This is unsurprising: nearly half of Luxembourg’s population is non-Luxembourgish, and the country’s Vivre Ensemble civic integration test and Sproochentest language exam are designed as accessible gateways rather than gatekeeping barriers.

VI. Tests, Barriers, and the View from the Exam Room

Eurostat counts the citizenships. It does not count the exams. But behind the 1.2 million figure is a vast, largely unmeasured infrastructure of testing: the language exams, the civic knowledge tests, the interviews, the integration courses, the waiting rooms, the document checks, the anxious months between application and decision.

Denmark, which falls outside the top five in absolute numbers but ranks among the most demanding in testing, requires nine years of residence, a B2-equivalent Danish exam, a 45-question civic test with an 80 percent threshold, a self-sufficiency clause, and a declaration of allegiance voted on by parliament. As we explored in our analysis of what citizenship tests reveal about national identity, the structure of a country’s test is as much a philosophical statement as a practical barrier.

Behind the 1.2 million figure is a vast infrastructure of testing, language exams, civic knowledge checks, and integration assessments that Eurostat does not count. The tests are invisible in the data. They are not invisible to the people taking them.

VII. What the Surge Means

Three forces are driving the numbers upward, and they are not going away.

The first is pipeline maturity. The 2015–2016 refugee arrivals, predominantly Syrian but also Afghan and Iraqi, have now reached the five-to-ten-year residency thresholds required across most of the EU. The Syrian naturalisation wave has been building for several years and will likely peak in the mid-2020s before tapering.

The second is legislative reform. Germany’s 2024 law was the most dramatic example, but it is part of a broader pattern. Countries across the EU have been adjusting their citizenship regimes — in some cases loosening them (Germany, Spain’s expanded bilateral agreements) and in others tightening them (Denmark’s 2021 values reform, the proposed Swedish language requirement). The net effect, so far, has been expansionary.

The third is dual citizenship acceptance. The old model — one person, one passport — is collapsing. Germany’s shift to unconditional dual citizenship removed the single largest structural barrier in the EU’s biggest country. As more states follow suit, the psychological cost of naturalisation drops. You no longer have to give something up to gain something new.

For testing, the implications are significant. More applicants mean longer waits for exam appointments, greater pressure on test administration systems, and — in countries where civic knowledge tests are gate-kept by limited sitting dates — growing backlogs. Germany has already experienced this. Denmark’s bi-annual test schedule, with its 6,000-candidate November sittings, is under similar strain.

The Invisible Exam Room

Eurostat’s data is about outcomes: citizenships granted, nationalities changed, legal bonds formed. What it cannot capture is the process — the months of study, the language courses, the practice tests at two in the morning, the conversations with lawyers, the administrative letters that arrive in a language you are still learning.

The 1.2 million new Europeans of 2024 are not a statistic. Each one passed through a gate. In some countries, that gate was a formality. In others, it was the hardest exam they have ever taken. As we track these numbers year by year at CivicLearn, the data tells us one thing with increasing clarity: the demand for citizenship in Europe is not softening. The pipeline is full, the laws are shifting, and the exam rooms — from the Einbürgerungstest centres in Berlin to the indfødsretsprøve halls in Copenhagen to the examen civique rooms in Lyon — have never been busier.

The 1.3 million mark is not far away. And when it arrives, the tests will still be there, waiting.
