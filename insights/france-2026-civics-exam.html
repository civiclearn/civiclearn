France’s New Civics Exam: What You Need to Know

The 2026 examen civique explained — from what changed and who needs it, to the unpublished questions that make France’s approach unlike any other in Europe.
CL
CivicLearn
Citizenship & Civic Testing • 20 years of test preparation across 15+ countries
The new exam asks forty questions. You can study for twenty-eight of them. The other twelve? That’s between you and the Republic.

For decades, France took a uniquely personal approach to deciding who belonged. There was no multiple-choice test, no published question bank, no standardized score. Instead, a candidate for French nationality would sit across from a prefecture official for a conversation — part oral exam, part interview, part civic catechism. Your guide was a slim government booklet called the Livret du Citoyen, a forty-page summary of French history, institutions, and republican values that was equal parts study aid and civic scripture. How thoroughly you needed to know it was anyone’s guess. The outcome depended on the official across the table, the prefecture you happened to live near, and, unavoidably, a measure of luck.

That system ended on January 1, 2026.

The arrêté of October 10, 2025, published in the Journal Officiel two days later, replaced the old assimilation interview’s knowledge component with a formal, digital, standardized exam: the examen civique. It is now required not only for naturalization, but for anyone applying for a multi-year residence permit or a ten-year resident card. The change is the most significant reform to France’s integration requirements in decades — and it creates a testing model that, as far as we can tell, exists nowhere else in the world.

•   •   •

I. What Existed Before

The old French system rested on two pillars: the Livret du Citoyen and the entretien d’assimilation (assimilation interview).

The Livret, published by the Ministère de l’Intérieur and still available today as a free PDF, covers the broad strokes any future French citizen should know: the values of the Republic (liberté, égalité, fraternité, laïcité), the key dates of French history, the structure of government from the President to the commune, and the rights and obligations of citizenship. It is well-written, concise, and — critically — not a test preparation tool in any strict sense. It outlines what you should know. It does not tell you what you will be asked.

The interview itself was a face-to-face meeting at the prefecture, typically lasting twenty to thirty minutes, in which an official would assess whether the applicant demonstrated sufficient knowledge of French society and genuine adhésion aux valeurs de la République. In practice, the experience varied enormously. Some prefectures were rigorous; others were a formality. The questions ranged from precise (“What happened in 1905?”) to philosophical (“What does laïcité mean to you?”). There was no score, no pass/fail threshold, no way to know in advance whether your preparation was adequate.

For naturalization candidates, the old French system was the educational equivalent of “dress code: smart casual.” Everyone interpreted it differently.

This is not to say the old system was bad. Many immigration lawyers and candidates remember it fondly as humane and personal. But it was inconsistent, subjective, and increasingly out of step with a political climate that demanded verifiable standards.

•   •   •

II. What Changed: The Law, the Decree, the Arrêté

The legal foundation was laid by the Loi du 26 janvier 2024 pour contrôler l’immigration, améliorer l’intégration — France’s 2024 immigration law, which among many provisions created the concept of a formal civic examination. A subsequent decree in July 2025 extended the requirement to naturalization applicants. And on October 10, 2025, the arrêté signed by Interior Minister Bruno Retailleau spelled out the details: the format, the themes, the pass rate, and the rules of the examination hall.

The effective date: January 1, 2026.

What this means in practice is that a reform conceived in 2024 legislation reached full operational detail only in October 2025, giving candidates, training organizations, and the examination bodies themselves roughly ten weeks to prepare for an entirely new system. For those following French immigration policy, this compressed timeline was characteristic.

•   •   •

III. Who Needs to Take It

The examen civique is now mandatory for three categories of applicants, each with its own exam “mention” (version):

MentionFor whomTypical context
CSPCarte de séjour pluriannuelleFirst-time multi-year residence permit (after initial 1-year CST)
CRCarte de résidentTen-year resident card
NaturalisationAcquisition of French nationalityBy decree (the most common path)

The three mentions share the same format — forty questions, forty-five minutes, digital QCM — but differ in the level of difficulty and depth of the questions. An attestation earned for the CSP level is not valid for a naturalization application; if your journey continues from residence to nationality, you take the exam again at the higher level.

Several important exceptions exist. Renewals of existing multi-year permits and resident cards do not require the exam. Beneficiaries of international protection (refugees, stateless persons) are typically exempt. EU citizens applying for naturalization do need to take it. And candidates with documented disabilities or chronic health conditions may receive accommodations or, in some cases, a full dispensation.

For CSP and CR applicants, the exam comes at the end of a mandatory 24-hour civic training course spread over four days — the reformed version of the contrat d’intégration républicaine. For naturalization candidates, no formal training course is required before the exam, though the same body of knowledge is expected.

•   •   •

IV. The Exam: Format and Structure

At a Glance

Format: Digital QCM (multiple-choice), on tablet or computer

Questions: 40 total — 28 knowledge questions + 12 mises en situation

Choices per question: 4 options, exactly 1 correct

Duration: 45 minutes maximum

Pass threshold: 80% (32 out of 40 correct)

Certificate validity: Indefinite (no expiration)

Retakes: Unlimited, at any time

Fee: €70 per sitting

Where: Approved CCI (Chambre de Commerce et d’Industrie) centers across France

The exam covers five thematic areas, and every candidate receives questions from all five, in roughly equal proportion:

ThemeQuestions*Covers
Principes et valeurs de la République~11Laïcité, liberté, égalité, fraternité, symbols, the motto
Système institutionnel et politique~6President, Parliament, government, EU institutions, local administration
Droits et devoirs~11Fundamental rights, tax obligations, parental authority, civic duties
Histoire, géographie et culture~8Key dates (1789, 1905, 1944…), regions, cultural landmarks
Vivre dans la société française~4Employment, healthcare, education system, daily civic life

* Approximate distribution for the naturalization mention. CSP/CR versions may vary.

The pass threshold of 80% is demanding. To put it in context: Germany’s Einbürgerungstest requires 17 out of 33 correct answers (roughly 52%). The UK’s Life in the UK test demands 75%. The United States civics test asks for 6 out of 10. France’s 80% bar places it among the strictest in Europe.

•   •   •

V. The Hybrid Model: Published Questions and Mises en Situation

This is where France’s approach becomes genuinely distinctive.

Of the forty questions on each exam, twenty-eight are questions de connaissances — knowledge questions drawn from a published, official list available on the government’s formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr website. These questions are fully transparent. You can download them, study them, memorize them. This is exactly how it works in Germany (with its bank of 310 questions), in the United States (128 questions), and in Spain (300 questions for the CCSE exam). In these countries, the contract between the state and the applicant is explicit: here is what we expect you to know; learn it, and you will pass.

But twelve of the forty questions are something entirely different: mises en situation.

The mises en situation are not published. They do not appear in any official study guide. They are not available on any government website. They are, by design, unknowable in advance.

These scenario-based questions present a concrete, everyday situation — at work, at school, in the neighborhood, at a public office — and ask you to identify the correct behavior or applicable rule. Your employer asks you to remove a visible religious symbol: what do you do? Your child refuses to attend school: what is your legal obligation? You receive a tax notice you don’t understand: what is the proper recourse?

The purpose is to test not whether you can recite that laïcité was established by the 1905 law, but whether you understand what laïcité means in the context of a real French workplace. Not whether you know the voting age, but whether you grasp what a citizen should do when they receive their carte électorale.

This creates a testing model that sits precisely between two established approaches. In the transparent model (Germany, US, Spain), everything is published and success is a function of memorization. In the opaque model (Denmark, Austria, Luxembourg to some extent), questions are drawn from a broad syllabus and nothing specific is published, making preparation a matter of deep comprehension. France has chosen both. Seventy percent of your exam is transparent. Thirty percent is a black box.

ModelCountriesPublished questions?Preparation approach
Fully transparentGermany, USA, SpainYes — all questions publishedMemorize the bank
Fully opaqueDenmark, AustriaNo — only the syllabusDeep comprehension of themes
Hybrid (France)France (since 2026)70% published, 30% unpublished scenariosMemorize + understand & apply

Whether this is a fair or wise design is debatable. Its defenders argue that the mises en situation prevent the exam from becoming a pure memorization exercise and ensure that candidates have genuinely internalized republican values, not merely learned to select the right answer. Its critics point out that scenario questions are inherently more subjective, harder to prepare for, and risk disadvantaging candidates with weaker French comprehension who might otherwise pass a straightforward knowledge test.

What is not debatable is that this structure makes the examen civique harder to prepare for than any fully transparent test. You can study every published question and get all twenty-eight right — but if you stumble on nine of the twelve mises en situation, you fail.

•   •   •

VI. The Exam Hall: What to Expect

The exam is administered at CCI-approved centers across metropolitan France and the overseas territories. Registrations opened in December 2025, and candidates book through the CCI Paris Île-de-France website.

On exam day, the atmosphere is closer to a professional certification than a casual interview. Your identity is verified against your titre de séjour or, for EU nationals, a passport or ID card. A photograph is taken before the exam begins. You sit at a computer or tablet. You have forty-five minutes. No documents, no phone, no communication with other candidates.

The rules are strict. Any candidate caught cheating or disrupting the exam is immediately expelled — and banned from retaking the exam for two years. France has, in effect, imported the anti-fraud rigor of national professional examinations into its civic testing regime.

One notable kindness: the certificate, once earned, never expires. Unlike in some countries (Australia’s citizenship test result lapses if the application isn’t completed within a set period), France’s attestation is valid indefinitely. And there is no limit on how many times you can retake the exam if you fail — though at €70 per attempt, repeated sittings add up.

VII. The Bigger Picture: Why France Chose This Path

The examen civique does not exist in isolation. It is part of a broader tightening of France’s integration requirements that includes raising the French language requirement for naturalization to B2 on the CEFR scale (previously B1), expanding the mandatory civic training, and making the exam a prerequisite not just for nationality but for long-term residence.

The political logic is straightforward. Since 2024, French immigration policy has moved toward what some legal scholars call the “moralization of the right to stay” — the idea that continued residence in France should be conditioned on demonstrable engagement with republican values, not merely on the absence of legal infractions. The examen civique is the most visible instrument of this shift.

The exam is not just a knowledge check. It is a political statement: France expects integration to be earned, measured, and proven — not assumed.

Whether this constitutes reasonable civic education or excessive gatekeeping depends largely on where you stand. From a comparative perspective, France’s new system is demanding but not unprecedented: Denmark’s indfødsretsprøve has long been considered the toughest civic exam in Europe, and several countries have moved toward formal testing in recent years. What makes France’s approach noteworthy is the combination of elements — the hybrid transparency, the multi-tier structure, the extension beyond naturalization to residence permits — and the speed with which it was implemented.

For the hundreds of thousands of foreign residents in France affected by this reform, the practical takeaway is blunt: the old world of subjective interviews and prefecture-dependent standards is over. In its place is a formal, standardized exam with a clear pass rate, published (and unpublished) questions, and consequences for failure. The Livret du Citoyen is still a fine document to read. But it is no longer enough.

VIII. How to Prepare: A Realistic Strategy

The good news is that the exam is not designed to be a trap. With structured preparation, the vast majority of candidates can pass. Here is what a sound approach looks like.

Start with the published questions

The knowledge questions are available on the official formation-civique.interieur.gouv.fr website, organized by theme. Download them, study them, and make sure you can answer every single one. These twenty-eight questions are your floor — if you get them all right, you only need four of the twelve mises en situation to reach 32/40.

Read the Livret du Citoyen

The Livret remains the best single-document overview of what the Republic expects a citizen to know. It is freely available from the Ministère de l’Intérieur. Read it not as a test-prep tool but as context — the framework within which both the knowledge questions and the scenario questions make sense.

Practice the mises en situation as a way of thinking

You cannot memorize the scenario questions because they aren’t published. But you can develop the logic they test. The underlying principle of every mise en situation is: what would a well-informed, law-abiding resident of France do in this circumstance? If you understand laïcité in the workplace, parental obligations in education, the basics of employment law, the tax system, and how to interact with public services, the scenarios become intuitive rather than mysterious.

Study in French

The exam is administered entirely in French. For CSP candidates, the language level of the questions is calibrated to approximately A2; for naturalization candidates, it assumes a solid B2. If your French comprehension is below the level needed for your mention, no amount of civic knowledge will help. The language barrier is often the real obstacle.

Take the right exam for the right purpose

Remember that each mention is a separate exam. A CSP attestation does not qualify you for a CR or naturalization application. If you know your long-term goal is citizenship, it may be worth investing preparation time at the naturalization level from the start.

•   •   •

The Door Is Different Now

In our global survey of citizenship testing philosophies, we categorized France as a “Fortress” — a country that treats citizenship as a prize to be earned through demonstrated cultural mastery. The examen civique confirms and deepens that classification. It is rigorous, standardized, and unambiguous in its expectations. It is also, in its hybrid design, uniquely French: both transparent and opaque, both systematic and philosophical, both rule-bound and deeply concerned with how you think, not just what you know.

The old door to French citizenship was a conversation. The new door is an exam room with a timer, a tablet, and a pass mark. The threshold is high — but it is visible, and that visibility is, for many candidates, an improvement. At least now you know exactly how tall the wall is.

The question is whether you can clear it.
