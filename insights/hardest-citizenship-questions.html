Every citizenship test has its trap questions. There's the date everyone confuses with a more famous one, the institution whose name sounds like its neighbour's, and the "obvious" answer that turns out to be wrong. We know which ones they are because we can see them: more than 4,000 people preparing for their citizenship test with CivicLearn have given nearly 13 million answers, and some questions trip up more than half of them.

We've collected the hardest questions from the practice banks of sixteen countries' citizenship tests. We picked the questions learners get wrong most often, along with the most surprising ones: obscure dates, trick wording and counter-intuitive facts.

Why these questions are so hard

The data shows the same patterns everywhere. The hardest questions are rarely the most obscure. They're the ones with a famous neighbour: Danish women got the local vote in 1908, but everyone remembers 1915. Romania signed its EU treaty in 2005, but everyone remembers joining in 2007. The Palais des Nations was built for the League of Nations, not the UN.

The second trap is near-identical names. Luxembourg has a Cour administrative and a Tribunal administratif. Sweden's private employers belong to Svenskt Näringsliv, while "Arbetsgivarverket", literally "the Employers' Agency", represents the state. In Geneva the Conseil municipal is the assembly and the Conseil administratif is the executive, while other cantons use similar names the other way round.

The third is common sense that isn't. Spain's health service is run by the regions, not the state. A majority of Swiss voters can approve a constitutional amendment and still lose. Australia's Commander-in-Chief is not the Prime Minister or the King, but the Governor-General.
