The World Citizenship Quiz

Fifteen real questions from eight countries' citizenship exams. One quiz. How many nations would let you in?

C
CivicLearn
Citizenship & Civic Testing · 20 years of test preparation across 15+ countries

Somewhere right now, a 34-year-old software engineer in Munich is staring at a question about Article 1 of the German Grundgesetz. A nurse in Sydney is trying to remember the colours of the Aboriginal flag. A retired teacher in Copenhagen is working through five questions on "Danish values" — knowing that getting even one wrong could fail the entire exam.

Every year, more than three million people worldwide sit a citizenship test. The questions range from the profound to the peculiar. The United States asks about the Louisiana Purchase. Switzerland once denied a man citizenship for not knowing what animals lived in his local zoo. Denmark's exam sparked a national scandal in 2019 when two of its multiple-choice answers turned out to be equally correct — a story we covered in detail.

As we explored in The DNA of a Citizen, the questions a country asks are never neutral. They reveal what a nation believes citizenship means. Germany tests whether you understand the constitutional weight of human dignity. Australia wants proof you accept that men and women are equal. France, launching its first-ever civic test in 2026, will present you with real-life dilemmas and ask what you'd do.

Below are fifteen real (or closely adapted) questions from the actual citizenship exams of eight countries. They are organized not by nation but by what kind of knowledge they test — your sense of history, your understanding of law, your knowledge of geography, your alignment with national values, and your familiarity with daily life. Three questions per section. Fifteen questions total.

At the end, we'll tell you which countries would have let you through the gate — and which would have turned you away.

What the Questions Reveal

If you breezed through the history section but stumbled on the values questions, you're in good company. Across the countries we track, the questions that trip people up most often aren't about memorizing dates — they're about internalizing principles. Denmark's notorious values gate, which requires four out of five correct answers on a separate subset, is the single biggest cause of failure on the Danish exam, even among candidates who easily pass the knowledge portion.

The geography and daily-life sections tend to split along lines of cultural familiarity. Most Australians know their flag is red, white, and blue. Fewer could tell you the colours of the Aboriginal flag, even though citizenship candidates are expected to. Most Germans understand Sonntagsruhe intuitively. For a newcomer, the idea that you can be fined for mowing your lawn on a Sunday is bewildering.

"The questions a country asks its would-be citizens are never just about knowledge. They are about belonging."

The United States, with its 97% pass rate and questions drawn from a published list of 128, designs its test for success. Denmark, with its 47% pass rate, ten questions pulled from current events that cannot be studied in advance, and a values gate with no margin for error, designs its test as a filter. Both are legitimate choices. Neither is accidental.

France enters this landscape in 2026 with the most intriguing innovation: mises en situation, scenario-based questions that ask not what you know but what you would do. When a colleague insults your religion, do you resign or report it? When someone collapses on the street, do you call 15 or 112? These questions have no official study guide. They assume that if you've been living in France — really living, as a participant in its society — you already know.

That, ultimately, is the thesis hidden inside every citizenship exam on earth. The test isn't asking whether you can become a citizen. It's asking whether you already are one — and just haven't received the paperwork yet.
