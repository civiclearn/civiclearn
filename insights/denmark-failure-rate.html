Why 53% Fail the Danish Citizenship Test

Five years of official SIRI data, the November curse, a question about the British fleet, and the structural reasons Denmark’s indfødsretsprøve defeats more than half its candidates.
CL

The saw-tooth pattern is unmistakable. Every November, the pass rate drops. Data: SIRI official press releases, 2020–2025.

On November 26, 2025, 6,156 people walked into language centers across Denmark, sat down at exam desks, and opened the indfødsretsprøve — the Danish citizenship test. They had forty-five minutes to answer forty-five multiple-choice questions about Danish history, democracy, economy, culture, current events, and values. Forty-five minutes later, 3,253 of them had failed. That is 52.8 percent — a majority.

This was not an anomaly. It was not a particularly hard exam year, nor a particularly easy one. It was, by the standards of Denmark’s citizenship test, perfectly ordinary. The indfødsretsprøve has been defeating more candidates than it passes for most of the past five years, and on November sittings — reliably, predictably, almost rhythmically — the failure rate climbs higher still.

No other Western democracy fails this many citizenship candidates this consistently. Not Germany, where roughly ninety percent pass. Not the United States, where the figure is above ninety-five percent. Not even the United Kingdom, where the much-maligned Life in the UK test has a pass rate around seventy percent. Denmark stands alone.

The question is why. And the answer is more interesting than you might expect.

•   •   •

I. The Numbers

SIRI — Styrelsen for International Rekruttering og Integration, the Danish Agency for International Recruitment and Integration — publishes the results of every citizenship test sitting. The exam is held twice a year, once in late May or early June and once in late November. Participants number in the thousands, and the results arrive a few weeks later in terse press releases: how many sat, how many passed, what percentage.

Two patterns leap from the data. The first is the overall scale of failure: across these eleven sittings, roughly 27,000 people passed out of approximately 53,000 who sat — a weighted average pass rate of just 50.9 percent. The second pattern is the shape. Look at the chart above and you will see a saw-tooth: up in summer, down in winter, up in summer, down in winter, without exception.

II. The November Curse

Every November sitting in the data set has a lower pass rate than the preceding summer sitting. Every single one. The gap is not small.

* November 2022 was higher than the preceding June 2022, but June 2022 itself was a historically weak summer sitting following the Nov 2021 values reform.

The average summer pass rate since 2021 is 56.4 percent. The average November pass rate over the same period is 43.6 percent. That is a twelve-point seasonal swing, repeating with remarkable consistency.

Why does November always hit harder? SIRI does not explain. The agency’s press releases rarely offer commentary; they report numbers and move on. But several plausible factors have been discussed in Danish media and among immigration lawyers.

The most common theory is compositional. Many candidates who fail the summer sitting retake the exam in November, meaning the November pool contains a higher proportion of repeat takers — people who already struggled once. First-time candidates, who statistically have higher pass rates, are more likely to attempt the spring exam, when the study material has been freshly published or updated. November, in this reading, is not a harder test; it is a harder crowd.

A second factor is the five aktuelle spørgsmål (current events questions). These cover news stories from the six months preceding the exam. The May/June exam draws its current events questions from the winter and spring news cycle — a period that tends to include the annual finanslov (government budget) debate, municipal elections, and other well-covered political events that are relatively easy to follow. The November exam, by contrast, pulls from the summer months, when news cycles are quieter and many candidates (and Danes) are on holiday. The current events that matter are harder to predict and harder to have noticed.

Whatever the cause, the pattern is real and has practical consequences. If you are preparing for the indfødsretsprøve, the data strongly favors sitting the May/June exam.

III. The 2021 Turning Point

Look again at the chart. The highest pass rate in our data is June 2021: 66.5 percent. The following sitting, November 2021, dropped to 40.7 percent — a fall of nearly twenty-six points. Something changed between those two exams, and it changed permanently.

In November 2021, the indfødsretsprøve was expanded from 40 to 45 questions. The original 35 knowledge-based questions and 5 current events questions remained, but a new category was added: five questions on Danish values. These questions cover themes such as freedom of speech, gender equality, the separation of religion and politics, and the relationship between the individual and the state.

Denmark did not just add five more questions. It added a second pass threshold: to pass the exam, you must get at least 36 out of 45 correct and at least 4 out of 5 values questions correct.

This is the structural innovation that makes the indfødsretsprøve unique among Western citizenship exams. It is not enough to accumulate a high total score. You can answer 40 out of 45 questions correctly — an 89% overall — and still fail if you get only 3 out of 5 values questions right. The values section operates as a separate gate within the exam.

Before November 2021, the historical average pass rate — as SIRI itself noted in its June 2022 press release — had been 54.5 percent across the preceding twelve sittings. After the reform, the average dropped to roughly 47 percent. The values questions did not merely raise the bar; they changed the nature of the exam. Where the pre-2021 test was a knowledge challenge, the post-2021 test is a knowledge challenge plus a values filter that candidates cannot afford to underestimate.

An analysis by Denmark’s Ministry of Immigration and Integration following the November 2021 exam found that 77.3 percent of candidates answered at least four of the five values questions correctly. That means the values gate, while significant, was not the sole driver of failure — but it was a new and decisive one for the roughly one-in-four candidates who could not clear it.

IV. The Architecture of Difficulty

To understand why the pass rate hovers around fifty percent, it helps to look at how the exam is built.

Exam Structure

45 questions, 45 minutes, multiple-choice (3 options per question)

35 knowledge questions based on the official 235-page Læremateriale, covering six chapters: Danish history, democracy, economy, international relations, cultural life, and thematic topics

5 current events questions drawn from news stories broadly covered in Danish media in the six months preceding the exam

5 values questions about freedom of expression, gender equality, the relationship between religion and politics, and similar themes

Pass requirement: Minimum 36/45 correct (80%) AND minimum 4/5 values questions correct

Fee: 905 DKK (~€121) per sitting

Frequency: Twice a year, at ~46 sprogcentre nationwide

No make-up exam: If you are ill, you wait six months and pay again

Several features of this architecture deserve attention.

The 80% threshold

Most citizenship tests in the world require 60 to 75 percent to pass. Germany asks for roughly 52 percent (17/33). The UK requires 75 percent (18/24). The US requires 60 percent (6/10). Denmark demands 80 percent — 36 out of 45 correct answers. You can miss a maximum of nine questions. In a forty-five-minute, high-pressure exam setting, nine is not a generous margin.

The unprepared questions

Ten of the forty-five questions — the five current events and five values questions — cannot be studied from the official material. The Læremateriale, for all its 235 pages and six chapters, covers only 35 of the 45 questions. For the remaining ten, candidates must rely on their general awareness of Danish news and their intuitive understanding of Danish societal norms. As we noted in our comparative analysis of citizenship testing models, this places Denmark firmly in the “Fortress” category: a country that demands not just book knowledge but cultural immersion.

The language barrier

The exam is administered entirely in Danish. There is no English-language option, no translation service, no simplified language version. For a candidate whose Danish is competent but not fluent, the difference between understanding a question and correctly understanding a question can be decisive. Analysis by the Ministry of Immigration found that candidates from Western countries pass at roughly 80 percent, while candidates from non-Western countries pass at roughly 40 percent — a gap that likely reflects both cultural familiarity and language proficiency.

The cost of failure

At 905 DKK per attempt (approximately €121), with no make-up exam for illness and no limit on retakes, the exam creates a meaningful financial pressure. A candidate who fails twice has spent nearly 2,000 DKK before their third attempt. Combined with the six-month wait between sittings, a single failure delays a citizenship application by half a year.

V. The Question About the British Fleet

Even well-designed exams make mistakes. In November 2019, SIRI’s exam included a question that would become briefly famous in Danish media: “Hvad erobrede briterne fra Danmark i 1807?” — What did the British conquer from Denmark in 1807?

The intended answer was A: Danmarks flåde — Denmark’s fleet. The bombardment of Copenhagen in 1807 and the seizure of the Danish navy is one of the foundational events of Danish national history, prominently covered in the study material.

But option B was De Vestindiske Øer — the Danish West Indies. And as DR Nyheder (the Danish Broadcasting Corporation’s news division) pointed out, the British had in fact occupied the Danish Caribbean colonies in 1807 as well. The question had two correct answers.

SIRI initially defended the question, arguing that the study material made the intended answer unambiguous. Two days later, the agency reversed itself, accepted both answers as correct, and acknowledged a second error in question 40 (for which all answer options were accepted). A third flagged error, in question 3, was deemed not to require correction.

SIRI apologized for the errors and thanked DR Nyheder for identifying them — a rare public acknowledgment by a government agency that the exam it administers was, on that occasion, flawed.

The episode is worth noting not because it was disastrous — the corrected scoring likely changed few outcomes — but because it illustrates a structural tension in the exam. The indfødsretsprøve tests mastery of a specific text (the Læremateriale), but history is not always as tidy as a 235-page study guide suggests. When a question’s “correct” answer depends on which version of history you learned, the exam’s claim to objectivity is tested alongside the candidates.

Following the incident, SIRI announced it would develop improved quality assurance procedures, including external expert review. The press releases for subsequent exams now routinely note that questions have been kvalitetssikret af eksterne eksperter — quality-assured by external experts.

The simplest way to understand Denmark’s position is comparison. Here are the approximate pass rates for citizenship tests in other developed democracies:

Denmark has the highest number of questions, the equal-highest pass threshold (tied with France’s new exam), and the only secondary sub-threshold in the values category. It administers the exam in Danish only, with no accommodations for non-native speakers. And it produces the lowest pass rate of any comparable test by a wide margin.

This is by design. Denmark’s citizenship requirements have been progressively tightened since the mid-2000s through a series of political agreements, typically between the center-right and the Danish People’s Party. The exam has been reformed multiple times — most recently with the 2021 values expansion — and each reform has raised the bar. The pass rate is not an accident or a failure of the education system. It is the intended output of a system designed to ensure that Danish citizenship is, in the words of a former immigration minister, something that “requires effort to achieve.”

•   •   •

VII. The Participation Surge

One detail in the data deserves its own section: the number of people taking the exam has grown dramatically. In June 2022, roughly 3,200 candidates sat the test. By November 2025, that figure had nearly doubled to 6,156. Across the most recent four sittings (November 2024 through November 2025), more than 24,000 people have attempted the indfødsretsprøve.

This surge reflects broader patterns. Denmark’s foreign-born population has grown steadily, and tightening requirements elsewhere in the immigration system — higher language thresholds, longer residency periods, stricter conduct requirements — may be pushing more residents to formalize their status through citizenship while the path remains open. The exam has also become a matter of public discussion, featured in media coverage that may have raised awareness among eligible candidates.

The growth in participation has not, however, improved the pass rate. If anything, the opposite: as the candidate pool has expanded, the pass rate has oscillated in its familiar range. More people are trying. The same proportion is failing.

What the 53 Percent Tells Us

A fifty-three percent failure rate is a number that can mean many things. To Denmark’s political establishment, it means the system is working: the test is demanding, it distinguishes prepared candidates from unprepared ones, and it ensures that new citizens possess genuine familiarity with Danish society. To immigration advocates, the same number tells a different story: that thousands of long-term residents who work, pay taxes, speak Danish, and have built lives in the country are being denied citizenship on the basis of a single examination — one that native Danes would not necessarily pass.

What the data reveals, beyond the politics, is a test that is structurally relentless. The 235-page study guide. The 80 percent threshold. The current events questions that require you to have been paying attention to DR Nyheder for the past six months. The values gate that can sink you even with a strong total score. The 905 DKK fee, the six-month wait, the absence of accommodations. Each element, individually, is defensible. Together, they produce a test that is, by any objective measure, the most difficult standardized citizenship exam in the Western world.

The indfødsretsprøve does not ask whether you are willing to become Danish. It asks whether you have already become Danish enough to prove it under exam conditions, in Danish, in forty-five minutes, on a November afternoon.

The doors to Danish citizenship remain open. The test can be retaken without limit. The study material is free, the old exams are published, and the path is clearly marked. But as the chart at the top of this page makes plain, the road is steep, the November wind is cold, and half the people who walk it do not reach the end.

At least, not on the first try.
