Canada's Regions

Canada is the second-largest country in the world, covering nearly 10 million square kilometres. Three oceans line Canada's borders: the Pacific Ocean to the west, the Atlantic Ocean to the east and the Arctic Ocean to the north. To the south, the border with the United States is the longest undefended international border in the world.

The Five Regions

Canada has many different geographical areas and five distinct regions:

  • the Atlantic Provinces;
  • Central Canada;
  • the Prairie Provinces;
  • the West Coast;
  • the Northern Territories.

The National Capital

Located on the Ottawa River, Ottawa was chosen as Canada's capital in 1857 by Queen Victoria. Today it is Canada's fourth-largest metropolitan area. The National Capital Region covers 4,700 km² around Ottawa and preserves the built heritage and natural environment of this historic area.

The Population of Canada

Canada has a population of about 42 million people. Most live in the large cities in the south, but there are also many rural and northern communities across the country.

The Atlantic Provinces

Thanks to their coastline and natural resources — fishing, farming, forestry and mining — the Atlantic Provinces have played an important part in Canada's history and development. The Atlantic Ocean brings cool winters and humid summers.

Newfoundland and Labrador

The most easterly point in North America, this province has a unique heritage tied to the sea. Newfoundland is the oldest colony of the British Empire and a major historic site. Offshore oil and gas production now drives much of the economy, while Labrador has immense hydro-electric resources.

Prince Edward Island

Canada's smallest province, known for its beaches, red soil and agriculture — especially potatoes. It is the birthplace of Confederation and the setting of the novel Anne of Green Gables. The Confederation Bridge links the island to the mainland.

Nova Scotia

The most populous Atlantic province. Its capital, Halifax, is Canada's largest east coast port. The economy is based on fishing, shipbuilding, forestry and offshore oil. Celtic and Gaelic traditions remain very much alive.

New Brunswick

Canada's only officially bilingual province, New Brunswick combines Loyalist and Acadian heritage. Its main industries are forestry, agriculture, fishing, mining and tourism. Fredericton is the capital, Saint John is the largest city and manufacturing centre, and Moncton is the main Francophone Acadian centre.

Central Canada

Quebec and Ontario are the economic and demographic heart of the country. More than half of Canadians live in cities and towns along the St. Lawrence River and the Great Lakes. It is Canada's industrial and manufacturing heartland.

Quebec

Nearly eight million people live in Quebec, most of them Francophones. Rich in natural resources, the province is a world leader in hydro-electricity, forestry, aerospace and pharmaceuticals. Montreal is Canada's second-largest city and a cultural centre of international stature.

Ontario

With more than 15 million people, Ontario is the most populous province, home to more than one-third of Canadians. Toronto is the largest city and Canada's main financial centre. Ontario has a multicultural population and a diverse economy — services, finance, manufacturing and agriculture. Niagara Falls and the Great Lakes are among its best-known symbols.

The Prairie Provinces

Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta form a vast region of farmland and energy resources. The climate is continental: cold in winter and hot in summer.

Manitoba

Manitoba's economy is based on agriculture, mining and hydro-electric power. Winnipeg, the capital, is home to St. Boniface, the largest Francophone community in Western Canada.

Saskatchewan

Once known as the “breadbasket of the world,” Saskatchewan has 40% of Canada's arable land and is the country's largest grain producer. It also has the world's richest deposits of uranium and potash. Regina is the capital and home to the training academy of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police; Saskatoon is the largest city.

Alberta

The most populous Prairie province, Alberta is rich in oil, natural gas and farmland. It is also known for its national parks — including Banff National Park, established in 1885 — and for its mountain and badlands landscapes. Edmonton is the capital and Calgary is a dynamic economic centre.

The West Coast

British Columbia

On the Pacific Ocean, British Columbia is known for its mountains and mild climate. The Port of Vancouver is Canada's largest and busiest, and a gateway to Asia. The economy is based on forestry, fishing, tourism and high technology. Victoria, the capital, is a tourist centre and the headquarters of the navy's Pacific fleet.

The Northern Territories

Nunavut, the Northwest Territories and Yukon cover one-third of Canada's land mass but have a population of only about 100,000. Mining for diamonds, gold, copper and zinc is important there. The climate is Arctic, with long, dark winters and short, bright summers.

Yukon

Made famous by the Klondike Gold Rush of the 1890s, Yukon is home to Mount Logan, Canada's highest mountain, and holds the record for the coldest temperature ever recorded in Canada (−63°C). Its capital, Whitehorse, is an important tourism and cultural hub.

Northwest Territories

Created in 1870, the Northwest Territories have Yellowknife as their capital, called the “diamond capital of North America.” More than half of the population is Aboriginal. The Mackenzie River, 4,200 km long, is the second-longest river system in North America after the Mississippi.

Nunavut

Nunavut, which means “our land” in Inuktitut, was established in 1999. About 85% of its population is Inuit. Its capital, Iqaluit, is the political and cultural centre of the Inuit. The territory is governed by consensus, and Inuktitut is an official language.

The Canadian Rangers

The Canadian Rangers, part of the Canadian Forces Reserves (militia), maintain Canada's presence and sovereignty in the Arctic. With their knowledge of the land, they contribute to national security and are a symbol of the resilience of Canada's North.
