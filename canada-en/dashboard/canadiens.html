Who We Are

Canada is known around the world as a strong and free country. Canadians are proud of their unique identity. We have inherited the oldest continuous constitutional tradition in the world, and we are the only constitutional monarchy in North America. Our institutions uphold a commitment to peace, order and good government, a key phrase in Canada's first constitutional document in 1867, the British North America Act. A desire for ordered liberty, enterprise, hard work and fair play has enabled Canadians to build a prosperous society in a rugged environment, from the Atlantic to the Pacific and up to the Arctic Circle, so much so that poets and songwriters have hailed Canada as the “Great Dominion”.

To understand what it means to be Canadian, it is important to know about our three founding peoples: the Aboriginal, the French and the British.

Aboriginal Peoples

The ancestors of Aboriginal peoples are believed to have come from Asia many thousands of years ago. They were living here long before the first European explorers arrived in North America. The vibrant and diverse First Nations cultures were rooted in religious beliefs about their relationship to the Creator, to the natural environment and to each other.

Aboriginal and treaty rights are set out in the Canadian Constitution. Territorial rights were first guaranteed through the Royal Proclamation of 1763 by King George III, which laid the basis for negotiating treaties with the newcomers – treaties that were not always fully respected.

From the 1800s until the 1980s, the federal government placed many Aboriginal children in residential schools to educate and assimilate them into mainstream Canadian culture. The schools were poorly funded and the students lived in hardship; some were physically abused. Aboriginal languages and cultural practices were mostly prohibited. In 2008, Ottawa formally apologized to the former students.

In today's Canada, Aboriginal peoples enjoy renewed pride and confidence, and they have made significant achievements in agriculture, the environment, business and the arts.

Today, the term Aboriginal peoples refers to three distinct groups:

Indian refers to all Aboriginal people who are not Inuit or Métis. In the 1970s, the term First Nations also came into use. Today, about half of First Nations people live in some 600 communities on reserves, and the other half live off-reserve, mainly in urban centres.

The Inuit, whose name means “the people” in the Inuktitut language, live in small, scattered communities across the Arctic. Their knowledge of the land, sea and wildlife enabled them to adapt to one of the harshest environments on earth.

The Métis are a distinct people of mixed Aboriginal and European ancestry. Most live in the Prairie provinces. They come from both French- and English-speaking backgrounds and speak their own dialect, Michif.

About 65 percent of Aboriginal people are First Nations, 30 percent are Métis and 4 percent are Inuit.

John Buchan, 1st Baron Tweedsmuir, was a very popular Governor General of Canada (1935–1940). He said that immigrant communities should keep their individuality and each make its own contribution to the national character. In his view, each could learn from the others and, while cherishing its own loyalties and traditions, also cherish the new loyalties and traditions that grow from their union. (Speech to the Canadian Club of Halifax, 1937.)

English and French

Modern Canadian society largely grew out of the English- and French-speaking Christian civilizations brought from Europe by settlers. English and French shape the daily reality of most people and are Canada's two official languages. The federal government is required by law to provide services in English and French throughout Canada.

Today there are 18 million Anglophones – people whose first language is English – and 7 million Francophones – people whose first language is French. While most Francophones live in Quebec, one million Francophones live in Ontario, New Brunswick and Manitoba, with a smaller presence in the other provinces. New Brunswick is the only officially bilingual province.

The Acadians are descendants of French colonists who began settling in 1604 in what are now the Maritime provinces. Between 1755 and 1763, during the war between Britain and France, more than two-thirds of the Acadians were deported from their homeland. Despite this ordeal, known as the “Great Upheaval,” the Acadians survived and kept their unique identity. Today, Acadian culture is flourishing and adds to the vitality of French-speaking Canada.

Québécois is the name for the people of Quebec, the vast majority of whom are French-speaking. Most are descendants of 8,500 French settlers who arrived in the 1600s and 1700s, and they keep a unique identity, culture and language. In 2006, the House of Commons recognized that the Québécois form a nation within a united Canada. One million Anglo-Quebecers have a heritage of 250 years and are a vibrant part of the fabric of Quebec society.

The way of life in English-speaking areas was largely shaped by hundreds of thousands of English, Welsh, Scottish and Irish settlers, soldiers and immigrants who arrived from the 1600s to the 20th century. Generations of pioneers and builders of British origin, along with other groups, worked, invested and endured many hardships to lay the foundations of our country. This partly explains why Anglophones (people who speak English) are generally called English Canadians.

Becoming Canadian

Some new Canadians come from places torn by conflict or war. Whatever those experiences, they do not justify bringing violent, extreme or hateful prejudices to Canada. Newcomers are expected to embrace democratic principles, such as the rule of law, when they become Canadian citizens.

Diversity in Canada

Since the 1800s, the majority of Canadians have been born in Canada. Even so, Canada is often called a land of immigrants, because over the past two centuries millions of newcomers have helped to build and defend our way of life.

Many ethnic and religious groups live and work side by side in peace as proud Canadians. The largest groups are the English, French, Scottish, Irish, German, Italian, Chinese, Aboriginal, Ukrainian, Dutch, South Asian and Scandinavian. Since the 1970s, most immigrants have come from Asian countries.

Non-official languages are spoken in many Canadian homes. Chinese languages are the second most common language spoken at home, after English, in two of Canada's biggest cities. In Vancouver, 13 percent of the population speaks a Chinese language at home; in Toronto, 7 percent.

The great majority of Canadians identify as Christians. The largest religious group is Catholic, followed by the various Protestant churches. The number of Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs and members of other religions, as well as people who say they have no religion, keeps growing.

In Canada the state has traditionally partnered with religious communities to promote social welfare, harmony and mutual respect; to provide education and health care; to resettle refugees; and to uphold freedom of religion, religious expression and conscience.

Canada's diversity includes gay and lesbian Canadians, who enjoy the full protection of and equal treatment under the law, including access to civil marriage.

Together, these groups share a Canadian identity and make up today's multicultural society.
