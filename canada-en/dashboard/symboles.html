Canadian Symbols

Canada has many important symbols – objects, events and people that have a special meaning. Together, they help explain what it means to be Canadian and shape our national identity.

The Canadian Crown

The Crown has been a symbol of the state in Canada for 400 years. Canada has been a constitutional monarchy in its own right since Confederation in 1867, during the reign of Queen Victoria. Queen Elizabeth II became Queen of Canada in 1952, marked her Golden Jubilee in 2002 and her Diamond Jubilee in 2012. Since September 8, 2022, King Charles III has been King of Canada.

The Crown is a symbol of government, including Parliament, the legislatures, the courts, police services and the Canadian Forces.

Flags in Canada

The current Canadian flag was raised for the first time in 1965. Its red-white-red pattern comes from the flag of the Royal Military College in Kingston, founded in 1876. Red and white, the colours of France and England since the Middle Ages, have been Canada's national colours since 1921.

The Union Jack remains Canada's official Royal Flag. The Canadian Red Ensign served as the Canadian flag for about 100 years. Each province and territory also has its own flag, reflecting its distinct traditions.

The Maple Leaf

Canada's best-known symbol, the maple leaf was adopted by French Canadians in the 1700s. It has appeared on military uniforms and insignia since the 1850s and is carved into the headstones of fallen soldiers buried in Canada and overseas.

The Fleur-de-lys

A symbol of French royalty since the 5th century (it is said the French king adopted it in 496), the fleur-de-lys also became the symbol of the colony of New France. It regained importance at Confederation and appeared on the Canadian Red Ensign. In 1948, Quebec adopted its own blue-and-white flag, based on the cross and the fleur-de-lys.

Coat of Arms and Motto

After the First World War, Canada adopted an official coat of arms and a national motto: A mari usque ad mare (Latin for “from sea to sea”). The arms contain symbols of England, France, Scotland and Ireland, as well as red maple leaves. Today they appear on bank notes, government documents and public buildings.

The Parliament Buildings

The towers, sculptures and stained glass of the Parliament Buildings reflect French, English and Aboriginal traditions. Built in the Gothic Revival style in the 1860s, they date from the reign of Queen Victoria. The Centre Block, destroyed by fire in 1916, was rebuilt in 1922. The Peace Tower, completed in 1927, honours the Canadians who died in the First World War. Its Memorial Chamber holds the Books of Remembrance, which record the names of service members who died serving Canada.

Provincial Legislatures

The provincial legislatures are architectural treasures. The National Assembly of Quebec is built in the French Second Empire style, while those of the other provinces are Baroque, Romanesque or neoclassical, reflecting the Greco-Roman heritage in which democracy originated.

Popular Sports

Hockey is considered Canada's national winter sport. Developed in Canada in the 1800s, it has been a passion for Canadians for generations. The Stanley Cup, awarded since 1892, goes to the championship team of the National Hockey League. The Clarkson Cup, established in 2005, is awarded in women's hockey.

Canadian football is the second most popular sport. Curling, brought by Scottish pioneers, and lacrosse, an ancient Aboriginal sport and the official summer sport, are also important. Soccer has the most registered players today.

The Beaver

A symbol of hard work and perseverance, the beaver was part of the emblem of the Hudson's Bay Company and in 1834 became an emblem of the Saint-Jean-Baptiste Society. It appears on the five-cent coin and on the coats of arms of Saskatchewan and Alberta and of cities such as Montreal and Toronto.

Canada's Official Languages

English and French are Canada's two official languages and important symbols of Canadian identity. Anglophones and Francophones have lived together in partnership for more than 300 years. To become a Canadian citizen, applicants aged 18 to 54 must show adequate knowledge of English or French (people aged 55 and over are exempt).

Adopted in 1969, the Official Languages Act has three main objectives:

  • Establish equality between English and French in Parliament, the Government of Canada and federal institutions.
  • Maintain and develop official language minority communities.
  • Promote the equality of English and French in Canadian society.

The National Anthem

O Canada was proclaimed the national anthem in 1980, a century after it was first sung in Quebec City in 1880.

Des plus brillants exploits.

The Royal Anthem

God Save the King is Canada's royal anthem, played to honour the Sovereign.

God save our gracious King!
Long live our noble King!
God save the King!
Send him victorious,
Happy and glorious,
Long to reign over us,
God save the King!

The Order of Canada and Other Honours

Canada recognizes outstanding citizens through a system of honours, orders and medals. The Order of Canada was created in 1967, for the centennial of Confederation.

The Victoria Cross

The Victoria Cross is the highest honour available to Canadians, awarded for outstanding bravery or self-sacrifice. Since 1854, 96 Canadians have received it.

  • Alexander Roberts Dunn — the first Canadian recipient, for his courage at Balaclava (1854).
  • William Hall — the first Black Canadian to receive it, for his heroism at the Siege of Lucknow (1857).
  • Filip Konowal — the first member of the Canadian Corps born outside the British Empire to receive it (1917).
  • Billy Bishop — flying ace, decorated during the First World War.
  • Paul Triquet — hero of the Battle of Casa Berardi (Italy, 1943).
  • Robert Hampton Gray — naval pilot, decorated posthumously in 1945.
