Federal Elections

Canadians vote in elections for the people they want to represent them in the House of Commons. In each election, voters may re-elect the same members of the House of Commons or choose new ones. Members of the House of Commons are also known as members of Parliament or MPs.

Under legislation passed by Parliament, federal elections must be held on the third Monday in October every four years following the most recent general election. However, the Prime Minister may ask the Governor General to call an earlier election.

Canada is divided into 343 electoral districts, also known as ridings or constituencies. An electoral district is a geographical area represented by one member of Parliament. The citizens in each electoral district elect one MP, who sits in the House of Commons to represent both their constituents and all Canadians.

Canadian citizens who are 18 years old or older may run in a federal election. The people who run for office are called candidates. In each electoral district, voters choose the candidate and political party they prefer. The candidate who receives the most votes becomes the MP for that electoral district.

Voting

One of the privileges of Canadian citizenship is the right to vote. To vote in a federal election or a federal referendum, you must be:

  • a Canadian citizen;
  • at least 18 years old on voting day;
  • on the voters’ list.

The voters’ lists are produced from the National Register of Electors, kept by a neutral agency of Parliament called Elections Canada. When an election is called, Elections Canada mails a voter information card telling you when and where to vote and what services are available.

Even if you do not receive a card, you can still have your name added to the voters’ list, including on election day.

Secret ballot

Canadian law guarantees the right to a secret ballot: no one can watch you vote and no one can demand to know how you voted. After the polls close, the ballots are counted and the results are announced publicly.

After an election

After an election, the leader of the party with the most seats in the House of Commons is invited by the Governor General to form the government and becomes Prime Minister. If that party holds at least half of the seats, it is a majority government; if it holds fewer than half, it is a minority government.

The Prime Minister chooses the Cabinet ministers, mostly from among the members of the House of Commons. The Cabinet prepares the budget, proposes most new laws and makes the main decisions. The other parties, called opposition parties, examine and criticize government policies and try to improve them. The opposition party with the most members is the Official Opposition (also called the Loyal Opposition).

Voting procedures

  1. Voter information card: confirms that you are registered and tells you when and where to vote.
  2. I did not get a card: call your local elections office or Elections Canada (1-800-463-6868).
  3. Advance voting or special ballot: available if you cannot vote on election day.
  4. On election day: bring your card and proof of identity to the polling station shown on it.
  5. Marking the ballot: mark an “X” beside the name of the candidate of your choice.
  6. Voting is secret: mark your ballot behind the voting screen.
  7. The ballot box: put your folded ballot in the ballot box.
  8. Results: the votes are counted and the results announced after the polls close.

Other levels of government in Canada

Local or municipal government plays an important role in citizens’ lives. Municipalities pass local by-laws and are responsible for services such as urban planning, garbage collection, snow removal, firefighting, emergency services, public transit and some social and health services.

Provincial, territorial and municipal elections are also held by secret ballot, but the rules differ. Find out about the procedures that apply in your own province, territory or municipality.

Responsibilities of the different levels of government

Level of government Elected officials Some responsibilities
Federal government Members of Parliament (MPs) National defence, foreign policy, citizenship, policing, criminal justice, international trade, Aboriginal affairs, immigration (shared), agriculture (shared), environment (shared)
Provincial and territorial government Members of the Legislative Assembly (MLAs), Members of the National Assembly (MNAs), Members of the Provincial Parliament (MPPs) or Members of the House of Assembly (MHAs) Education, health care, natural resources, highways, policing (Ontario and Quebec), property and civil rights, immigration (shared), agriculture (shared), environment (shared)
Municipal (local) government Mayor or Reeve, Councillors or Aldermen Social and community health, recycling programs, transportation and utilities, snow removal, policing, firefighting, emergency services

First Nations have band chiefs and councillors who are responsible for housing, schools and other services on reserves. A number of Aboriginal organizations represent First Nations, Métis and Inuit people in their dealings with the federal and provincial governments.
