Modern Canada

Modern Canada

Trade and Economic Growth

A medical researcher

Post-war Canada enjoyed record prosperity and major progress. Agreements such as the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), now the World Trade Organization (WTO), helped ease the restrictive trade policies applied around the world during the Great Depression. The discovery of oil in Alberta in 1947 launched Canada's modern energy industry. In 1951, for the first time, a majority of Canadians could afford adequate food, shelter and clothing. Between 1945 and 1970, Canada drew closer to the United States and other trading partners, and the Canadian economy became one of the most successful among industrialized nations. Today, Canadians enjoy one of the highest standards of living in the world – sustained by their hard work and by trade with other countries, especially the United States.

As Canada grew more prosperous, so did its ability to support social programs. The Canada Health Act ensures common elements and a basic standard of coverage; the federal government introduced unemployment insurance (now called “employment insurance”) in 1940; Old Age Security dates back to 1927; and the Canada Pension Plan and Quebec Pension Plan have existed since 1965. The provinces and territories also provide publicly funded education.

International Engagement

Toronto's business district, Canada's financial capital

Like Australia, New Zealand and other countries, Canada gradually became more independent and is now able to make major contributions internationally. The Cold War began when several liberated countries of Eastern Europe became part of a Communist bloc controlled by the Soviet Union under the dictator Joseph Stalin. Canada joined other Western democracies to form the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO), a military alliance, and joined the United States to create the North American Aerospace Defence Command (NORAD).

Canada joined international organizations such as the United Nations (UN). It took part in the UN operation defending South Korea in the Korean War (1950–1953), with 500 dead and 1,000 wounded. It has also taken part in many UN peacekeeping missions in places as varied as Egypt, Cyprus and Haiti, as well as other international security operations, including in the former Yugoslavia and Afghanistan.

Canada and Quebec

In the years after the war, French Canadians flourished both socially and culturally. The 1960s in Quebec were an era of rapid change known as the “Quiet Revolution.” Many Quebecers sought to separate from Canada. In 1963, Parliament set up the Royal Commission on Bilingualism and Biculturalism, which led to the Official Languages Act (1969), guaranteeing French and English services from the federal government across Canada. In 1970, Canada helped found La Francophonie, an international association of French-speaking countries.

The Quebec sovereignty movement gained strength but was defeated in a provincial referendum in 1980. After much negotiation, the Constitution was amended in 1982 without Quebec's agreement. Although the sovereignty movement was defeated again in a second referendum in 1995, Quebec's autonomy within Canada is still debated today and remains part of the dynamic that continues to shape our country.

A Changing Society

As social values evolved over more than fifty years, Canada became a more flexible and open society. Many people benefited from wider access to high school and post-secondary education, and more and more women entered the professions.

Most Canadians of Asian descent had in the past been denied the right to vote in federal and provincial elections; the last of them to gain it, Japanese Canadians, finally did so in 1948. Aboriginal people were granted the vote in 1960. Today, every citizen aged 18 or older may vote.

Canada welcomed thousands of refugees fleeing Communist oppression, including about 37,000 who escaped Soviet tyranny in Hungary in 1956. After the Communist victory in 1975 at the end of the Vietnam War, many Vietnamese fled, and more than 50,000 of them found refuge in Canada.

The idea of multiculturalism, a result of 19th- and 20th-century immigration, gained new momentum. By the 1960s, one-third of Canadians were of neither British nor French origin, and they were proud to keep their distinct cultures within the Canadian mosaic. Today, diversity enriches Canadians' lives, especially in our cities.

Arts and Culture in Canada

Cirque du Soleil

Canadian artists have a long history of achievement that makes their fellow citizens proud. Coming from every region, they reflect and define our culture and forms of creative expression, and they have won acclaim both at home and abroad.

Canadians have made important contributions to literature in both English and French. Novelists, poets, historians, educators and musicians have had a considerable influence on culture. Notable writers include Stephen Leacock, Louis Hémon, Sir Charles G. D. Roberts, Pauline Johnson, Émile Nelligan, Robertson Davies, Margaret Laurence and Mordecai Richler. Musicians such as Sir Ernest MacMillan and Healey Willan are known in Canada and around the world. Writers such as Joy Kogawa, Michael Ondaatje and Rohinton Mistry have broadened the Canadian literary landscape.

The Pine, by Tom Thomson

In the visual arts, Canada is perhaps best known historically for the Group of Seven, founded in 1920, which developed a style of painting that captured the rugged wilderness landscape. Emily Carr painted the forests and Aboriginal artifacts of the West Coast. The Automatistes of Quebec, notably Jean-Paul Riopelle, were pioneers of modern abstract art in the 1950s. Louis-Philippe Hébert of Quebec was a famous sculptor of great historical figures. Kenojuak Ashevak laid the foundations of modern Inuit art with her etchings, prints and soapstone sculptures.

With its network of regional theatres and internationally renowned performing companies, Canada has built a strong and lasting reputation in the performing arts.

The films of Denys Arcand have been successful in Quebec and across the country, and have won international awards. Directors Norman Jewison and Atom Egoyan are other great names of Canadian film. Canadian television productions have large followings.

(Left to right) Donovan Bailey; Chantal Petitclerc; Terry Fox; Wayne Gretzky

Canada also excels in sports, both amateur and professional, and every province and territory has produced star athletes and Olympic medallists. Basketball was invented by Canadian James Naismith in 1891. Talented Canadians play in many major leagues, and Canadian teams dominate the world in ice hockey – Canada's national sport. Donovan Bailey set a world record and won two gold medals in sprinting at the 1996 Summer Olympics, while Chantal Petitclerc became a world Paralympic champion in wheelchair racing. One of the greatest hockey players of all time, Wayne Gretzky, played for the Edmonton Oilers from 1979 to 1988.

In 1980, Terry Fox, a British Columbian who lost his right leg to cancer at the age of 18, began a run across the country called the “Marathon of Hope” to raise money for cancer research. He became a hero to Canadians. Sadly, he was unable to finish his run and eventually lost his battle with cancer, but his story lives on through annual fundraising runs held in his name. In 1985, another British Columbian, Rick Hansen, circled the globe in a wheelchair to raise funds for spinal cord research.

Mark Tewksbury, Olympic gold medallist and leading advocate for gay and lesbian Canadians

Canada's scientific and technological discoveries are known around the world and have changed the way the world communicates and does business. Marshall McLuhan and Harold Innis were pioneering thinkers. Other countries recognize Canada for its excellence in science and research, and world-class students, researchers and entrepreneurs come here to do research in medicine, telecommunications and other fields. Since 1989, the Canadian Space Agency and Canadian astronauts have taken part in space exploration, often using the robotic arm designed and built in Canada. Canadian scientists Gerhard Herzberg (a refugee from Nazi Germany), John Polanyi, Sidney Altman, Richard E. Taylor, Michael Smith and Bertram Brockhouse have won the Nobel Prize.

Catriona Le May Doan carries the flag after winning a gold medal in speed skating at the 2002 Winter Olympics

Canadian football is a popular game that differs in several ways from American football. Professional teams in the Canadian Football League (CFL) compete for the championship trophy, the Grey Cup, donated by Governor General Lord Grey in 1909

Sir Frederick Banting of Toronto and Charles Best discovered insulin, a hormone used to treat diabetes, which has saved 16 million lives worldwide.

Great Canadian Discoveries and Inventions

Many Canadians have made great discoveries and inventions. Some of the most famous are listed below.

  • Alexander Graham Bell — conceived the idea of the telephone at his summer home in Canada.
  • Joseph-Armand Bombardier — invented the snowmobile, a lightweight winter vehicle.
  • Sir Sandford Fleming — invented the worldwide system of standard time zones.
  • Mathew Evans and Henry Woodward — together invented the first electric light bulb and sold the patent to Thomas Edison, who marketed it and went down in history.
  • Reginald Fessenden — contributed to the invention of radio, sending the first wireless voice message in the world.
  • Dr. Wilder Penfield — was a pioneering brain surgeon at McGill University in Montreal, known as “the greatest living Canadian.”
  • Dr. John A. Hopps — invented the first cardiac pacemaker, used today to save the lives of people with heart problems.
  • SPAR Aerospace / National Research Council — invented the Canadarm, the robotic arm used in space by the U.S. space shuttle.
  • Mike Lazaridis and Jim Balsillie — of Research In Motion (RIM), a wireless communications company known for its most famous invention, the BlackBerry.
